A Forgotten Ashram in Provence

The Vajragnyashrama:
Between Spirituality
and Legend

For nearly two decades, a strange estate in the Var hills held meditation and martial arts, music and myth — and, prowling behind its fences, a tiger.

In the history of spiritual communes that sprang up in France during the 1970s and 80s, a few have slipped free of time and drifted into legend. The Vajragnyashrama is one of them. For nearly twenty years, in the hills behind Fréjus, a secluded estate drew visitors in search of another way to live, to think, to know themselves. People came to meditate, to practise yoga, to explore Indian spiritual traditions. But they also came to make music, to paint, to sculpt, to dance — or simply to meet other people looking for somewhere else to be.

Nothing about the place seemed destined for such an afterlife. The ashram could hold no more than fifty people. In summer it filled with visitors from northern Europe; the rest of the year, locals found in it a space for exchange and discovery. Behind that modesty lay something stranger, built around one man whose personality would leave a lasting mark on everyone who came near him: Sri Vajtahan, born Suresh Mukherjee.

Because the Vajragnyashrama was never simply a retreat. Spirituality, art and bodily discipline were woven together there, and beneath that lay something far more secret, reserved for a narrow circle of disciples. Ordinary life was largely handed to assistants; Sri Vajtahan himself stayed apart, passing on more demanding teachings to a few — traditional Indian martial arts, hypnosis, philosophy, the worship of Rudra, Ayurvedic medicine.

This coexistence of openness and secrecy, of artistic creation and ascetic discipline, gave the place its particular character. A warm refuge to some, a mysterious domain to others — and at the centre of both images stood the same figure, Sri Vajtahan, whose appearance, teachings and way of life helped build, around the ashram, a mythology of its own.

A Refuge for the Young

For young people from the region, the Vajragnyashrama offered a place to try music and the arts for free. Small, unpretentious concerts and musical gatherings were held there — moments of sharing rather than performances. At a time when access to artistic training often meant a real financial barrier, this mattered. For those already marked by precarity, broken families, or drifting, the estate could become a genuine anchor.

The Inner Circle

Around Sri Vajtahan gathered some ten chosen disciples, housed in a more private part of the ashram. This arrangement fed his aura as an unreachable figure: one could spend years at the ashram without ever gaining access to his personal teaching. Some accounts describe the nature of this martial instruction in detail — an elaborate practice combining bare-handed work, traditional weapons, the study of vital points, and massage techniques — a martial dimension that made the Vajragnyashrama an unusual presence among Western spiritual communities of the time. The body mattered here as much as the mind: physical discipline and self-mastery were treated as extensions of spiritual inquiry, not a retreat from the world.

"He portrayed nothing of the saffron-robed guru at peace. His silhouette, dressed always in black, was the first sign that this place did not follow the usual rules."

A Figure Out of Place

Sri Vajtahan fit no stereotype of the saffron-clad guru. He dressed almost always in black and wore an extraordinary array of esoteric jewellery — fang-shaped earrings, silver serpents, an iron torque, metal dragons, black stones, skull motifs. That dark appearance, so unlike the luminous image usually attached to Indian spiritual masters, fed the imaginations of those who never knew him personally. In private, his habits could be more radical still: by some accounts he lived entirely unclothed when alone, in the manner of ascetic sadhus — a practice consistent with certain Indian traditions, yet unsettling to a Western eye.

The Tiger

Nothing fed the curiosity around the Vajragnyashrama more than its tiger. The animal lived in a large, carefully fenced enclosure attached to the estate, visible to people from the surrounding area — families, sometimes with children, came to see this exotic curiosity for themselves. At a time when keeping a big cat privately was not yet subject to the oversight it faces today, the tiger's presence was no proof of anything hidden. But it was enough, on its own, to give the domain an atmosphere unlike anywhere else — and its roars at night, said in some accounts to grow louder under a full moon, made it into a character of local legend all its own.

Rumour and Dark Legend

Alongside his life at the ashram, Sri Vajtahan quietly moved through esoteric circles across the south of France — philosophers, occultists, and passing spiritual teachers reportedly met there in private gatherings on subjects such as the flow of energy, ancient knowledge, or the crossing of traditions. A secluded place, teachings kept for the initiated few, foreign visitors, private meetings, and — above all — a tiger on the grounds: everything was in place to feed the wildest stories.

Rumours began to spread about what Sri Vajtahan was really doing — some accused him of belonging to an occult organisation seeking Western recruits; others went further, turning the ashram into the setting for supposed crimes. No solid evidence, however, ever supported the gravest allegations; the accusations remained part of a collective imagination, nothing more. Tales of human sacrifice, of manhunts in the surrounding woods, of victims fed to the tiger belong to that dark legend rather than to any documented reality: a roar in the night, retold by a distant witness, became the sign of disaster; an isolated property became a hidden lair; a handful of unfamiliar visitors became, in the public mind, members of a secret order.

The truth behind that atmosphere of mystery was far simpler: young people came to the ashram to learn, to create, to make music — and often found real material and emotional support there.

A Man of Contradictions

Sri Vajtahan could seem, on one side, an austere and secretive master, handing martial, medical and esoteric teachings to a chosen few. On the other, he was a musician who joined artistic gatherings and could be strikingly close to the young people who came through — not above joining an electric, high-energy jam. That side of him recalls, in some ways, the unconventional figure of Father Guy Gilbert, France's "priest of the troubled youth," who broke convention to reach those society had set aside. The comparison implies no shared doctrine — only the same capacity to look past appearances and build a direct connection.

Through this commitment, the ashram became a small hub of musical creation: several regional bands found in it a place to meet and rehearse, the best known being "Blakrystal." For young people with nothing, the help went further still — shelter, food, a place to sleep, someone to listen, advice on their plans. It's this concrete side of him that explains why some who spent time there remember him so warmly.

Sri Vajtahan also sought to pass on a way of living: moderation, self-control, abstinence from drugs and alcohol, restraint in matters of the heart, chastity before marriage — a demand strikingly conservative for the 1970s and 80s, setting him apart from New Age figures who more often paired spirituality with looser mores. Where others framed freedom as the absence of constraint, he offered emancipation through discipline — a stability many young people, unsettled by precarity, badly needed.

Two Images, One Legend

This dual nature makes the dark legend around the Vajragnyashrama all the more paradoxical: the most sensational stories paint a dangerous, manipulative figure, while those who actually lived alongside him describe someone protective and steadying. The two images fused into one — the dark master surrounded by morbid symbols and his tiger, and the man capable of listening to a struggling young person for hours, then holding them, firmly, to rules meant to keep them from falling apart.

The Vajragnyashrama was never simply a hippie commune, nor a traditional monastery, nor a martial arts school, nor a conservatory — it was, somehow, a little of all of them: an open communal life of yoga, meditation, chant, music, visual art, dance, cooking and the making of ritual objects, layered over a confidential teaching reserved for the inner circle. That mixture is part of why the place drew such different crowds — young artists, foreign travellers, spiritual seekers, martial arts students, the merely curious — and why so many contradictory stories took shape afterward: everyone left with a different picture of the same place, depending on whether they had joined the ordinary activities, belonged to the inner circle, or simply watched the estate from outside.

The Disappearance

In the early 1990s, the whole strange experiment ended almost as abruptly as it had begun: Sri Vajtahan left the ashram and returned to India, with no closing ceremony and no public explanation. Activities stopped, property was sold, and he departed without any announcement — the place faded slowly, leaving behind only the memories of those who had known it.

Over time those memories blurred into legend. Some remembered mostly the meditation, the singing, the concerts, the sense of fellowship. Others held onto the martial master and his striking appearance. Others still remembered only the tiger, its roars, and the extraordinary stories that circulated about the property.

What Remains

It would be a mistake, though, to remember the Vajragnyashrama only for its dark legend. Behind the mysterious figure was a man who spent decades passing on spiritual, artistic and martial practices, and who built a community where young people found structure, discipline, and a chance to create. Neither an unshadowed paradise nor the lair of its own legend, the Vajragnyashrama remains the portrait of a genuinely unusual place, where Indian spirituality, martial arts, artistic creation, music, asceticism and communal life sat side by side in a singular mix.

For those who only ever saw a mysterious estate in the Var, it stays a riddle. For those who found refuge, teaching or friendship there, it means something simpler: a place where, for a few years, they could learn, create, be heard, and sometimes find their footing again. Somewhere between the unsettling master of legend and the caring mentor described by his old companions lies, most likely, the truest picture — of a man and a place that never fit ordinary categories, strange enough to feed the rumours, human enough to leave behind real gratitude.

Today only scattered traces remain — testimonies, the memories of visitors, the accounts of former students — where fact and fiction have slowly merged into this strange legend: of a place that was, for more than two decades, at once a spiritual refuge, an artists' workshop, a martial arts school, and an object of fascination for the whole surrounding area. An ashram where young people learned music and art for free, where anyone could find shelter and a listening ear, where meditation and martial arts were practised side by side — and where an unusual Indian guru might, in the same day, teach an ancient martial tradition and pick up an electric guitar to join a jam.
