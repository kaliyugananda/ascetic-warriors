MALMATIYYA


From the 9th century in Nishapur, in Khorasan, emerged an absolutely radical Sufi path: the Malamatiyya, the “path of blame”. Its adherents, often drawn from the spiritual elite, deliberately chose to attract public condemnation in order to destroy the ego and conceal their true piety from the eyes of the world. God alone must know their devotion; men must see only the apparent sinner.


The most extreme branches of the Malamatiyya, particularly in the 10th and 11th centuries, pushed this logic to the abyss. They frequented taverns and places of debauchery, ostentatiously drank wine, neglected public prayer, insulted the ulama, dressed provocatively or wandered naked. Some accounts mention hashish consumption, transgressive relationships, even openly blasphemous acts to ensure universal contempt. Their body became an instrument of shame: discreet self-mutilations, hidden fasts to the point of exhaustion, wearing chains under their clothes to maintain constant suffering that no one must suspect.


The darkness of these radical malamati lies in their merciless double life: in private, intense meditation, ceaseless dhikr, rigorous asceticism; in public, the appearance of total depravity. This concealment demands terrifying inner violence: they had to endure insults, blows, social exclusion without ever revealing their true nature. Some masters imposed trials lasting years on their disciples: living as a notorious drunkard, a thief, or a debauchee, while preserving absolute inner purity.


Their provocation is not gratuitous: it aims to break all attachment to reputation, honor, human approval. Malamati texts speak of a “death before death”: the ascetic must socially kill his ego to be reborn in God. This path directly influenced later Qalandar and Haydari, who made public the transgressions the Malamati kept secret. Accused of hypocrisy by the orthodox, they replied that only public shame guarantees sincerity before God.


Disappeared as an organized movement by the 12th century, absorbed by other tariqas or become too dangerous, the extreme Malamatiyya left a dark legacy: the idea that true holiness may require the appearance of the blackest sin. It reminds us that the mystical quest can plunge into the darkness of dissimulation and social self-destruction, where the ascetic becomes a monster in the eyes of men in order to become a saint in the eyes of God alone.
