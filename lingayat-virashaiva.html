LINGAYAT / VIRASHAIVA


In the 12th century, in Karnataka and the Deccan, there erupted a revolutionary Shaivite movement of radical social and spiritual violence: the Lingayat, or Virashaiva (“heroes of Shiva”), founded by the minister and poet Basava. Violently rejecting the caste system, idols, brahmins, and Vedic rituals, they proclaimed that every devotee bearing the personal linga (phallic symbol of Shiva) is equal and directly united with the divinity, without intermediary.


Their history is that of a mystical utopia turned open war: Basava, vizier to King Bijjala, transformed the court into an egalitarian community, welcoming untouchables, women, and artisans as priests. This subversion culminated in a bloody revolt: assassinations of brahmins, destruction of orthodox temples, massacres during the fall of Kalyana (1167), where thousands perished in flames and swords. Radical Virashaiva justified violence as divine purification: killing a caste oppressor was a sacred act.


The darkness of the extreme Lingayat lies in their merciless asceticism and mystical intolerance. Some followers practiced fasts until voluntary death (sallekhana influenced by Jainism), or ritual self-mutilations to prove detachment. Their vacana poets sang the destruction of body and social world as the path to Shiva: “Burn the castes, burn the scriptures, burn me myself.” Marginal branches meditated in charnel grounds, used human ashes, or adopted ritual nudity in defiance of norms.


Their rebellion was both spiritual and armed: bands of devotees attacked Brahmanical villages, imposing the wearing of the linga by force, executing those who refused. This egalitarian fury terrified neighboring kingdoms, who suppressed the movement in blood. Basava himself ended in exile or mystical suicide according to legends. Persecuted, the Lingayat survived in closed communities, retaining a visceral mistrust of orthodox Hinduism.


Even today, the Lingayat (several million) claim separate status from Hinduism, and certain fringes preserve a revolutionary fervor. Their somber legacy reminds us that the quest for divine union can demand the total destruction of the social order, where mystical equality is paid for in blood and absolute rejection of the profane world. The Virashaiva embody a spirituality where revolt becomes prayer, and violence a hymn to divine freedom.
