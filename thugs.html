THUGS


Between the 13th and 19th centuries, India harbored one of the most organized and terrifying criminal brotherhoods in history: the Thugs, or Phansigars (“stranglers”). Worshipping Kali in her most destructive aspects—Bhavani, the black Durga—these hereditary bands practiced ritual murder by garrote (rumal) as a human sacrifice intended to appease the goddess and ensure their prosperity.


Their history is that of a secret network spanning the entire subcontinent: family clans initiated from childhood, coded signs, divine omens to choose victims, precise division of spoils according to sacred rules. They infiltrated merchant caravans, gained their trust over days, then, at the ritual signal, simultaneously strangled all targets without spilling a drop of blood—for Kali, according to their belief, refused blood offerings. Bodies were buried in mass graves or thrown into wells, after a final rite where the consecrated rumal was purified.


The darkness of the Thugs lies in the perfect fusion of mysticism and criminality: every murder was a sacred act, carried out with ritual coldness. Accounts from survivors or confessions extracted by the British describe chilling scenes: entire families strangled amid the laughter of Thug children, pilgrims murdered after sharing the sacred meal, women and children included without distinction. Estimates suggest they killed between 500,000 and one million people over several centuries, turning India’s roads into a vast blood-soaked altar dedicated to the Terrible Mother.


Their organization was quasi-military: recognition by secret signs, strict hierarchy, inviolable oath of silence. Gurus interpreted omens (an owl, a jackal, a sneeze) to authorize or forbid operations. The goddess herself, they claimed, protected her servants: no Thug could be caught as long as he respected the ritual rules. This conviction made them terrifyingly bold, operating sometimes within miles of Mughal or British garrisons.


The repression campaign led by William Sleeman in the 1830s–1840s ended their reign: thousands arrested, detailed confessions, mass executions. Yet their legacy endures as the darkest testimony of the fusion between devotion and criminality: a religion where ritual murder was not a means, but the very end of the spiritual quest. The Thugs remind us that the dark face of the goddess may demand not only the destruction of the ego, but that of countless lives, transforming divine love into an ocean of silent death.
