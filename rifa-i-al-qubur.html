RIFA‘I AL-QUBUR


At the heart of the vast Shia necropolises of Iraq, where tombs stretch like an infinite sea of stone and dust, spectral figures emerge that blur the boundaries between the living and the dead: the Rifa‘i al-qubur, or “Rifa‘i of the tombs”. This designation, far from being a formal branch of the Sufi order Rifa‘iyya founded in the 12th century by Ahmad al-Rifa‘i, applies to a handful of radical ascetics who pushed their tariqa’s ecstatic practices into the darkest abysses. Inspired by the Rifa‘i legacy of ritual ecstasy and confrontation with pain, these isolated individuals—often seen as relics of a marginal Sufism—have made cemeteries their eternal dwelling, transforming death into a living sanctuary where spirituality merges with the horror of decomposition.


The origins of the Rifa‘i al-qubur trace back to the popular and heterodox ramifications of the Rifa‘iyya order, which spread in Iraq and Syria from the 13th century onward. The main order, known for its intense vocal rituals and physical demonstrations—such as body perforation without apparent pain—always maintained an extreme fringe where asceticism crossed the limits of orthodoxy. In the 18th and 19th centuries, in Iraq’s Shia regions, certain Rifa‘i dervishes, influenced by qalandari and malang traditions, withdrew into major cemeteries such as Wadi al-Salam in Najaf or Bab al-Saghir in Damascus. These places, housing millions of graves, became refuges for a spiritual quest that embraced ritual necrophagy: not morbid perversion, but total immersion in death as a path to ego annihilation and divine union. Rejected by official religious authorities, who considered them majnūn—possessed madmen—or bid‘a (heretical innovators), they survived on the margins, tolerated by cemetery guardians who feared them as intercessors with jinn and wandering souls.


The darkest practices of the Rifa‘i al-qubur reveal an asceticism that flirts with utter abjection, turning body and mind into a battlefield against the illusion of life. Their daily existence is total immersion in decay: they sleep directly on graves, wrapped in the putrid vapors of recent burials, inhaling the smell of decomposition like incense that purifies the soul. At night, they invoke the dead and invisible entities through shouted dhikr, repeating “Ya Mawlana! Ya Mawlana!” until fainting, their voices mingling with the funeral winds sweeping the stone alleys. For sustenance, they use unwashed human skulls as bowls, collecting offerings from pilgrims or scattered funeral remains—a gesture symbolizing the absorption of death itself. Their bodies, marked by countless piercings and scarifications, become living altars: they drive spikes, knives, or nails into the flesh, claiming pain is the key to unlocking the doors of the beyond. Coated in ashes from pyres or graves, they wear necklaces and bracelets of human bone fragments taken from abandoned tombs as talismans against the illusion of the material world.


This confrontation with darkness does not stop at physical suffering; it plunges into psychic abysses. Under the influence of hashish or opiates consumed in massive quantities, they engage in nocturnal trances where visions of Shia hells blend with demonic invocations, calling upon jinn for secret pacts promising divine revelation at the price of madness. Their chants, resounding in the silence of necropolises, evoke a sinister communion with the departed, where wandering souls are summoned to testify to the vanity of life. Rejected by society, they subsist on charity from pilgrims who fear them as hidden saints or cursed sorcerers, offering alms in exchange for spiritual protection against malevolent forces. This liminal existence, between sacred and profane, renders them untouchable: religious authorities tolerate them reluctantly, fearing that expulsion might unleash occult forces upon the sacred cemeteries.


At the dawn of the 21st century, the Rifa‘i al-qubur persist as vestiges of a forgotten Sufism, their number reduced to a handful of individuals aged thirty-five to seventy, mainly confined to Wadi al-Salam. Sporadic testimonies—a viral 2016 video showing an ascetic dancing naked with a skull at dawn, or clandestine 2018–2019 reports—reveal their precarious survival in an Iraq scarred by conflict. A French convert, nicknamed Abu al-Qubur, shared in 2023 his eight-month experience among them, describing nights haunted by terrifying visions and a discipline that erased every trace of humanity. Yet this somber asceticism is not gratuitous: it aims at total ego annihilation, where death becomes the gateway to divine freedom, as proclaimed in their ritual adage: “The grave is the house of the living, and pain its eternal guardian.”


The Rifa‘i al-qubur, these shadows of the cemeteries, embody the extreme limit of Rifa‘i Sufism, where the spiritual quest merges with the horror of decay. Their legacy, though marginal and condemned by orthodoxy, reminds us that the mystical path can plunge into the darkest abysses, transforming death not into finitude, but into a bitter nectar of illumination. Amid the swirling smoke of Iraq’s necropolises, they endure as living relics, defying the world of the living through their intimate union with darkness.
