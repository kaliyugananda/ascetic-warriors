NIZARI FEDAYIN


Between the late 11th and early 13th centuries, in the inaccessible fortresses of Persia and Syria, a Nizari Ismaili Shia branch thrived—known in the West under the terrifying name of “Assassins” or “hashashin”. Led by the legendary Hasan-i Sabbah and later by his successors, these devotees of the Old Man of the Mountain turned their mystical devotion into a formidable political weapon: the fedayin, young fanatical initiates, sacrificed themselves by assassinating Sunni princes, generals, and high dignitaries, often at the cost of their own lives.


Their story is that of a secret holy war against the Seljuk and Abbasid empires. Holed up in impregnable castles such as Alamut, they developed an esoteric theology in which the hidden Imam held absolute truth, and political murder became a sacred act that freed the killer’s soul. The fedayin, selected from adolescence, underwent ruthless ascetic training: isolation, fasting, meditation on death, and controlled consumption of hashish to transcend fear.


The darkness of the Assassins lies in their cult of voluntary sacrifice: every mission was a mystical suicide. The fedayin would infiltrate for months, gain their target’s trust, then strike in public with a consecrated dagger, calmly awaiting death afterward. Crossed accounts describe chilling scenes: a smiling young man plunging his blade into a vizier’s heart before being slaughtered by the guard, convinced he was going straight to the paradise promised by the Old Man of the Mountain.


Their asceticism was not physical like that of the Aghori or Qalandar, but psychic and martial: total renunciation of earthly life, iron discipline, absolute obedience to the Imam. According to legend (and possibly reality), hashish served to give them paradisiacal visions, reinforcing their conviction that death on mission was an immediate gateway to divine ecstasy. This fusion of mysticism and political terrorism makes them one of the coldest and most calculated expressions of sacred violence.


Destroyed by the Mongols in 1256, the Nizaris survived in discreet communities (today led by the Aga Khan). Their legacy remains as the most chilling testimony of a spirituality in which divine love demanded ritual murder and self-sacrifice, turning the ascetic into a living weapon in the service of hidden truth.
