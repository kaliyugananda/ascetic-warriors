HAYDARIYYA / JAWBARIYYA


In the 14th century, in the tormented lands of Timurid Iran and Safavid Iraq, there emerged a dervish brotherhood of unparalleled violence and marginality: the Haydariyya, often confused or allied with the Jawbariyya. Inspired by the mystic Qutb al-Dīn Haydar (died around 1221), these ascetics distinguished themselves through an asceticism of extreme brutality: they drove rusty irons and skewers into their flesh, wore heavy chains that lacerated the skin, shaved themselves completely (skull, beard, eyebrows, eyelashes), and lived in permanent nudity or semi-nudity, defying all human modesty.


Their history is that of a radical drift from qalandarism: rejecting any structure, they roamed in armed bands, commanding respect through the terror they inspired. Safavid and Ottoman chronicles describe them as hordes of raging madmen, consuming hashish and opiates to the point of dementia, dancing with chains clashing against their open flesh, shouting invocations that chilled the blood of passersby. The Jawbariyya, an even more extreme branch, were accused of openly criminal practices: organized theft, violent extortion, ritual assassinations, and even symbolic cannibalism during collective trances.


The darkness of the Haydari lies in their cult of pain as a gateway to the divine. Their scarifications and piercings were not decorative: irons pierced through muscles, chains weighed dozens of kilos, wounds were deliberately infected to maintain a state of permanent suffering. They meditated in cemeteries, sleeping among graves, inhaling the vapors of decomposition, claiming that putrefaction was the perfume of truth. Some accounts report public acts of self-mutilation: tearing off strips of skin, ingesting ground glass, walking on embers that left charred flesh.


Their violence extended to the outside world: armed bands attacking caravans, villages, or Sunni mosques (depending on the era), sowing panic under the pretext of punishing religious hypocrisy. The Jawbariyya in particular were described as mystical criminals: ritual thefts offered to their patron saint, murders committed in trance, bodies marked with scars forming bloody invocations. Rejected by all orthodox Sufi orders, they survived thanks to the superstitious fear they inspired: they were said to be invulnerable to weapons, capable of cursing to death or possessing spirits.


This immersion in horror and criminality was not depravity: it was a mystical method to annihilate the ego through absolute pain and shame. The Haydari considered that only the total destruction of the social and physical body allowed union with the Absolute. Their legacy persists among contemporary Jalali malangs in Pakistan and Afghanistan, where one can still find survivors wearing irons driven into their flesh and living in total marginality from humanity. Modern studies highlight this terrifying duality: a path where holiness is born from the most brutal self-destruction, where divine love manifests through the most merciless violence against oneself and the world.
