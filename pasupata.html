PASUPATA


The Pāśupata, one of the oldest Shaivite sects in India, emerged at the turn of the first centuries CE as a radical manifestation of Hindu asceticism, where devotion to Śiva took the form of a merciless quest for liberation through transgression and suffering. According to tradition founded by Lakulīśa, an enigmatic master of the 2nd century often depicted as an incarnation of Śiva holding a staff and a lemon, this school draws its name from Pāśupati, the lordly aspect of Śiva as master of beasts—a title symbolizing divine dominion over the animal passions of the human soul. Influenced by emerging tantric currents and Sāṃkhya philosophical speculations, the Pāśupata developed a doctrine in which asceticism is not peaceful retreat but immersion into the abysses of humiliation and pain, transforming the body into a battlefield against cosmic illusion.

The history of the Pāśupata is rooted in the obscure strata of post-Vedic India, where texts such as the Pāśupata Sūtra, attributed to Lakulīśa, outline a spiritual path divided into five progressive stages, culminating in mystical union with Śiva. Lakulīśa, whose iconography dominates temples in Gujarat and Rajasthan, is often portrayed as a naked yogi, his body marked by austerity, teaching in isolated caves or cremation grounds. Flourishing between the 2nd and 10th centuries, this sect spread from Kashmir to the Deccan, influencing kings and royal courts while maintaining a radical ascetic fringe that defied Brahmanical social norms. Epigraphic inscriptions, such as those from Mathurā or Udayagiri, attest to their presence from the 4th century, where royal donations supported their monasteries, revealing a duality between elite patronage and marginal practices.

At the heart of their doctrine lies an asceticism of abyssal darkness, where the darkest practices aim to break the chains of pāśa—the bonds that tie the soul to the cycle of rebirths. The Pāśupata engaged in ritual imitation of animals, behaving like beasts to ridicule the human ego: growling, crawling, or imitating the cries of wild animals in public spaces, they invited contempt and insults as a means of purification. This voluntary humiliation, often practiced naked and covered in funeral ashes, culminated in symbolic acts of violence: striking the body with stones, lacerating the flesh, or feigning madness to attract the mockery of passersby. These rites, described in medieval commentaries as “acts of grace” (anugraha), transformed suffering into a gateway to divine ecstasy, where physical pain eroded the illusions of the material world.

Even more sinister were their meditations in places of death: the Pāśupata haunted cemeteries and cremation fields, anointing themselves with human fat or ashes from funeral pyres to absorb the essence of decomposition. These necrophagous practices, evoking communion with the chthonic forces of Śiva, included nocturnal invocations where the ascetic, plunged into total darkness, confronted wandering spirits and inner demons. Their violence was not always inward: hagiographic accounts report confrontations with orthodox authorities, where the Pāśupata, armed only with their fervor, defied Vedic priests through provocative acts, sowing ritual chaos to impose their vision of raw and merciless devotion. This aggressiveness, though symbolic in essence, forged a reputation for fanaticism that made them suspect in the eyes of elites while attracting seekers of accelerated liberation through abjection.

The influence of the Pāśupata extended far beyond their era, irrigating later tantric currents and ascetic orders such as the Kāpālika or Nāth Yogis, where warrior asceticism and transgression became pillars. Their somber legacy persists in the practices of modern Naga Sadhus, who perpetuate a masked ritual violence under asceticism, reminding us that the spiritual quest can plunge into darkness to emerge purified. Contemporary studies highlight this duality: a sect that, through its darkness, contributed to the evolution of Shaivism, transforming violence into a tool of divine transcendence.
