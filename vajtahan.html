The Ashram of the Var,
or the Ambiguity of the Sacred

At the heart of the austere landscapes of the Var region, where the mistral seems less to blow than to whisper, there once extended an ashram whose very existence bordered on a geographical and cultural anomaly. Hidden among gnarled pines and light-scorched earth, the place appeared both deeply rooted in its surroundings and strangely detached from any contemporary reality. At its helm stood a singular figure, Sri Vajtahan—the pseudonym of Suresh Mukherjee—whose authority, from the 1970s to the early 1990s, asserted itself with an intensity that is difficult to define, oscillating between spiritual charisma and a more obscure form of influence.

The ashram itself, designed to host a limited number of initiates—never more than fifty—cultivated a deliberate form of withdrawal. Behind its ochre stone walls, a multifaceted discipline was taught: yogic practices, deep meditation, the recitation of mantras, but also artistic forms of expression such as painting, sculpture, and the crafting of ritual objects imbued with ancient symbolism. This apparent diversity, often interpreted as a sign of openness, in fact concealed a more demanding, almost doctrinal coherence, in which each activity contributed to a single system of inner transformation.

During the summer months, the site attracted a predominantly Northern European population—particularly Scandinavian and German visitors—drawn by a diffuse reputation circulating within alternative circles. This word of mouth, never explicitly formulated yet constantly sustained, promised an extraordinary spiritual experience. And yet, while some visitors spoke of genuine elevation, others left with a lingering unease, as though they had glimpsed, beyond the luminous discourse, something more troubling, something difficult to name.

This ambiguity seemed inseparable from the very person of Suresh Mukherjee Vajtahan. Contrary to the conventional image of a spiritual master, he rarely appeared in public. Collective teachings were delivered by his assistants, while he himself withdrew into the most remote parts of the estate. There, surrounded by a carefully selected inner circle of disciples, he imparted teachings of a very different nature: Indian martial disciplines reputed to be highly confidential, exegesis of complex Vedic texts, and advanced practices of Ayurvedic medicine, where the boundary between healing and toxicity seemed deliberately explored.

His appearance contributed to this aura of tension. Always dressed in black, he broke with the visual codes typically associated with Eastern spiritual figures. His tall, almost spectral silhouette moved with an economy of gestures that suggested not so much serenity as absolute control. In private, he adopted even more radical practices, moving about naked in the manner of certain extreme ascetics. His body bore the visible marks of prolonged austerity, but also more enigmatic signs, such as discreet scarifications whose meaning eluded observers.

Another element reinforced the singular—indeed unsettling—character of the place: the presence of a tiger, kept in a large enclosure out of sight. Invisible from the outside, the animal nonetheless made itself known through its nocturnal roars, particularly audible on nights of the full moon. This animal presence, at once fascinating and profoundly dissonant within the Provençal landscape, contributed to an atmosphere of latent tension. At a time when legislation remained vague regarding the keeping of such predators, this peculiarity formed part of a broader ambiguity: everything in this universe seemed legal without ever appearing entirely legitimate.

The ashram also occasionally served as a meeting place for individuals from various esoteric spheres. Marginal philosophers, occultists, and itinerant spiritual masters gathered there in strictly private settings for confidential conferences. These exchanges, whose content remained inaccessible to most, addressed complex themes: the circulation of energies, ancient knowledge, and hybridizations between traditions. Accounts diverge as to their effects: some participants spoke of profound transformations, while others seemed to emerge altered in a more indefinable way, as though some part of themselves had been irreversibly displaced.

In this context, rumors soon began to emerge and proliferate. One of the most persistent suggested that Suresh Vajtahan was not merely a spiritual teacher, but the representative of an occult organization of Indian origin whose objectives extended far beyond the realm of personal quest. According to these accounts, the ashram may have served as a point of observation and recruitment, targeting influential individuals or those particularly receptive. Although no tangible evidence ever confirmed these allegations, they became a lasting part of the collective imagination surrounding the place.

These suspicions were reinforced by connections with a certain Wong Wei, an apparently discreet figure whose unofficial reputation linked him to Sino-Vietnamese criminal networks operating in southern France. Behind a façade of respectability, he was said to have overseen activities involving counterfeiting, precious metal trafficking, and various clandestine circuits. His proximity to Suresh Vajtahan, never explained, raised many questions. The regular presence of his grandchildren, Henri Wong and Katie Cheng, within the ashram—where they stood out for their discipline and martial skills—only heightened the impression of a convergence between worlds that should, in principle, have remained separate.

Then, in the early 1990s, everything vanished almost without transition. Suresh Vajtahan brought the ashram’s activities to an end, disposed of his assets, and left the region without any public statement. His return to India was as discreet as his departure from it had been mysterious. From that point on, any trace of his existence seemed to fade progressively, as though absorbed into a methodical silence.

Today, only fragments of memory remain: partial accounts, hesitant testimonies, impressions difficult to articulate. The ashram itself survives only as a diffuse imprint upon the landscape and within the minds of those who once approached it. Yet among them persists a troubling conviction: that an ancient, complex, and profoundly ambivalent phenomenon took shape in those hills for more than two decades, before dissolving without explanation—leaving behind a lingering sensation, a mingling of cold incense, sandalwood… and a threat never entirely dispelled.
