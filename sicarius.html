SICARII


In the 1st century CE, in Judea under Roman domination, there arose a Zealot faction of fanatical and sacrificial violence: the Sicarii. Named after the curved dagger (sica) they concealed under their garments, these men regarded Roman occupation as an absolute profanation of the Temple and the chosen people. Their response was a secret holy war blending ritual assassination, public terror, and self-sacrifice.


Their history is recounted by Flavius Josephus with fascinated horror: they infiltrated crowds during major festivals in Jerusalem, approached their targets—Roman prefects, collaborating high priests, Hellenized Jewish notables—and stabbed them in broad daylight before vanishing into the panic. The murder was not merely political; it was a sacred act, a purification through spilled blood to hasten the coming of the Messiah and divine liberation of Israel.


The darkness of the Sicarii culminated in their absolute refusal to surrender. Besieged at Masada after the fall of Jerusalem in 70 CE, 960 of them—men, women, children—chose collective suicide over Roman capture. According to Josephus, they drew lots to kill one another, the last man taking his own life, preferring death to enslavement and profanation. This act transformed the fortress into a giant altar, where an entire life became an offering to God.


Their violence also extended to moderate Jews: massacres of villages suspected of collaboration, the destruction of Ein Gedi where 700 women and children were killed to punish alleged treason. This merciless messianic purity isolated them even from other Zealots, who viewed them as uncontrollable extremists. Their asceticism was not bodily like that of the Aghori, but total: renunciation of earthly life, acceptance of torture and death as the ultimate testimony.


Vanished with the fall of Masada in 73 CE, the Sicarii left a dark legacy: the first organized expression of mystical terrorism, where ritual murder and self-sacrifice became weapons in the service of absolute truth. They foreshadow the Nizari fedayin and the Thugs, reminding us that the divine quest can demand not only the destruction of the enemy, but the total annihilation of self and kin.
